Turning a Blank Notebook Into Your Child's First Story

Creative Writing·September 2026

A blank page can feel exciting to one child and intimidating to another. If your young writer freezes up the moment you hand them a notebook, the fix usually isn't more instruction — it's a smaller starting point.

Start with a sentence, not a story

"Write a story" is a big ask. "Write one sentence about a character who finds something surprising in their backpack" is a doorway. Most children will keep going once they're past the first line — the hardest part is almost always starting.

Give them a character before a plot

Plot pressure is what stalls a lot of young writers. Instead, ask who the story is about first: their name, what they're afraid of, what they want. Once a character feels real to a child, a plot tends to follow naturally, because they start asking "what would this character do?"

Use prompts that leave room to wander

Open-ended prompts work better than ones with a single correct direction. "The map was wrong" invites a dozen different stories. "Describe the map" invites one.

Celebrate the weird ideas

Unexpected, silly, or strange story turns are often a sign of genuine creative engagement, not a distraction from "real" writing. Praising the imaginative leap — even if the story wanders — keeps children willing to take creative risks instead of second-guessing themselves.

Keep the notebook low-stakes

No corrections, no grading, no "let's fix the spelling" mid-story. A story notebook works best as a space that feels different from schoolwork — one where the goal is simply getting ideas down.

This is exactly the thinking behind My Story Writing Adventure — built to make that first blank page feel like an invitation rather than a test.
